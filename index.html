WIUT Fintech Hackathon · Team 855BB00D

Twenty thousand alerts, one question: which ones matter?

Every day a financial monitoring unit in Uzbekistan gets automated alerts about customers' transactions. Specialists read each one and either dismiss it or escalate it for investigation. Most are dismissed. We were handed 10 million transactions and asked to learn the difference. This is the story of how we looked, what we found, and the model that came out of it.

By Sayidahror Mirzaakhmedov, Muzaffar Nurillayev and Farrux Chorikulov

Prologue

How we attacked the problem

We started with a simple rule: look before you model. We spent the first hours with nothing but plots and counts, and only then let a model near the data. Each alert (signal_id) comes with the customer's transaction history, and the task is to rank alerts by how likely they are to be escalated. We turn each history into one feature row per alert, then train boosted-tree ensembles with 5-fold stratified cross-validation. Out-of-fold ROC-AUC is the only metric we trust.

Chapter 1 · The case files

What was on the desk

Think of each alert as a case file: a date, a verdict (for the training cases), and a folder of the customer's past transactions. Some folders are thin, with a single transaction. Others are more than two thousand pages thick.

There are two relational tables: one row per alert, and many transaction rows per alert. A transaction has a timestamp, a direction (kirim incoming / chiqim outgoing), a type (karta card, bank_otkazmasi bank transfer, naqd cash, xalqaro international), and miqdor_indeksi, a standardized size index. Every alert has at least one transaction, and each history covers up to 180 days before the alert date.

Chapter 3 · Rewinding the clock

What happens in the months before an alert?

Our first instinct was that trouble announces itself: a burst of activity just before the alert. So we lined up every history on its alert date and counted transactions for each day before it. The clock told a different story, plus one we didn't expect at all.

The pre-alert "pile-up"

Transactions in the final 24h, by seconds before midnight of the alert date. Nearly all of them land in the last 5 minutes.

The surprise: a crowd at the door. 7.9% of all transactions (about 39 per alert) are timestamped 0–300 seconds before the alert date, compared with only 1–3 transactions per alert on an ordinary day. Their mix differs from normal history too: mostly small incoming card payments. We treat this "pile" as a separate batch and compute features on it and on the true history separately, so it doesn't blur the behavioral statistics.

Chapter 5 · Every channel has its own scale

A big card payment is a small wire

Transaction type is the strongest driver of size: card < bank transfer < cash < international. So a "large" amount has to be judged against its own type, which is why we build amount statistics per direction × type cell and not only globally.

Chapter 7 · The magnifying glass

The clue that was hiding between the bins

Our first model plateaued at AUC 0.630, and its favorite feature was oddly a_min, the smallest transaction an alert contains. Why would the tiniest payment matter? We re-drew the size histograms at 0.004 resolution, 60× finer than the charts above, one direction × type at a time. At the very bottom of the scale, the smooth curve broke into narrow, flat-topped bands with sharp edges. Pairs of them sit side by side and point in opposite directions.

Tiny incoming bank transfers, zoomed in

Share of transactions that belong to escalated alerts, per 0.004-wide size bin. Dashed line: the 17% base rate.

Alerts containing each band

Escalation rate among alerts with at least one transaction in the band (base rate 17.2%).

The breakthrough. Alerts containing a micro incoming bank transfer at −2.892…−2.872 escalate 27.4% of the time. Alerts with one in the band just 0.02 lower escalate only 12.5%. Bands like these exist in several direction × type cells. They are invisible to ordinary histograms, and even to gradient boosting, which splits a feature into at most 255 buckets, far too coarse for a band 0.02 wide. In anti-money-laundering terms, they resemble the small "test" transfers used to check that an account works before real money moves.

Rather than hand-pick bands, we let the data find them. Every transaction is placed in a (direction, type, 0.02-wide size) bucket. Each bucket's escalation log-odds is estimated out-of-fold, so an alert never sees its own label, and each alert sums the evidence from its transactions. That one feature reaches AUC ≈ 0.59 on its own, the strongest single clue in the dataset.

What the line-up taught us

No simple statistic separates the classes well: the best reaches AUC ≈ 0.57, and even the band evidence only reaches ≈ 0.59. The signal is spread across many weak, mostly additive cues. That later explained why a sparse linear model beat gradient boosting.

Chapter 9 · From clues to features

Turning what we saw into features

Every chapter above left us a clue, and every clue became a family of features:

  1. Hidden amount bands (Chapter 7): out-of-fold escalation evidence per (direction, type, 0.02-wide size) bucket, summed per alert, plus explicit counts for the six bands we found by eye.
  2. Direction × type grid: count, mean, max and volume (Σ eindex) for each of the 8 cells, because size depends on type and the outgoing-transfer gap is the strongest raw cue.
  3. Pile vs history split: the same statistics computed separately on the pre-alert batch and on the true history, plus the differences between them.
  4. Recency windows (1, 3, 7, 14, 30, 60, 90, 180 days): counts, volumes, in/out ratios, and each window's rate relative to the 180-day baseline, to capture build-up before the alert.
  5. Distribution shape: quantiles, skew, kurtosis, and the share of very small or very large amounts, reflecting the small-amount over-representation.
  6. Sequence and pass-through: gaps between transactions, bursts (max per day), incoming-then-outgoing within 2h/24h with matching amounts, and repeated amounts.

What we tried that didn't work, and why that mattered

  • A transaction-level model. We scored every single transaction (each inheriting its alert's verdict, out-of-fold by alert) and summarized per alert. It reached only AUC ≈ 0.53 and didn't help the ensemble (0.6285 vs 0.6300). In hindsight it should have found the bands, but its 255 coarse buckets per feature were too wide. That failure is what sent us back to zoom in.
  • The same evidence trick on time. Buckets by day-before-alert, hour of day, and time gap between transactions: all ≈ 0.50. The planted signal lives in amounts, not timing.
  • Linking customers across alerts. We checked whether any transaction appears under more than one alert, in train or test, which would reveal repeat customers. None does, so every alert has to be judged on its own history.
  • Early stopping on the scored fold. It made CatBoost look like 0.639 when the honest figure was 0.635. We removed it: we would rather report a smaller true number.
Chapter 10 · The verdict machine

Model & validation

Validation is 5-fold stratified CV repeated over 3 seeds, and every number on this page is out-of-fold. A model that tries to tell train alerts from test alerts scores AUC 0.50, meaning the two sets are indistinguishable, so CV is a faithful preview of the leaderboard.

Our biggest modeling surprise: a sparse L1 logistic regression on quantile-normalized features beat gradient boosting. With hundreds of weak, additive clues and only 14k alerts, the simpler model overfits less. The final prediction is a rank blend of three models, with weights chosen on out-of-fold predictions.

Epilogue

What we'd tell the monitoring desk

  • Look closer than the histogram. The strongest clue was hiding in narrow bands of tiny transfers only 0.02 wide. Some push toward escalation (up to 30%), others toward dismissal (as low as 8%). Finding them, and switching to a sparse linear model that can add up many small clues, lifted us from 0.630 to 0.65 AUC.
  • Use the ranking as a triage queue. Reviewing the top 30% of alerts by our score catches – of all escalations, versus 30% for a random order.
  • Two kinds of evidence do the heavy lifting: how large each kind of transaction is (direction × type), and the hidden bands. Removing either costs real accuracy. Recency, timing and calendar features add almost nothing on top.
  • Escalation is otherwise a weak, spread-out signal. Many small additive cues matter more than any single rule, which is why a sparse linear model beat boosted trees.
  • The clearest behavioral cue is size relative to transaction type. Escalated customers move money in smaller pieces, especially outgoing bank transfers, while doing slightly more transactions overall.
  • The data has a pre-alert batch of transactions stamped in the final minutes before the alert date. Separating it from the real history makes the features cleaner.
  • Time of day, weekday and calendar month are essentially uninformative. Train and test come from the same distribution, so CV is reliable.
